What this is

Haven Daily is a short brief, published every morning, on what affects the Port of Rotterdam and what it means for the people who plan cargo through it. It collects port and logistics news, keeps what matters for Rotterdam, and turns it into at most five items, most severe first. Each item says what happened, links to the original source and gives an interpretation of the impact for a planner. Quiet days get a shorter brief. It is never padded.

It is a personal project, not a company tool, and it is not affiliated with the Port of Rotterdam Authority or any company in the port. Haven means port in Dutch and a safe place in English.

How a brief is made

  1. Collect, around 06:45 Amsterdam time. A scheduled job reads the sources listed below and keeps only items it hasn't seen in the last 14 days.
  2. Filter. Fixed rules go first: items older than 48 hours, port notices that ended more than 12 hours ago and clearly off-topic headlines (football, for example) are dropped. Items from Rotterdam-specific sources are kept. An AI model checks the rest: is this relevant to a planner working with the Port of Rotterdam? When unsure, it keeps the item.
  3. Summarise. A second AI model writes a two-line summary, a planner impact and a severity for each kept item, following the rules below, and picks its place on the chart and its "affects" tags from fixed lists.
  4. Status. The job reads the port status: weather warnings, the Rhine water level at Kaub and the strike flag.
  5. Publish. The brief is saved and the site goes online straight away. A day is never skipped.

Every step is automatic. Nobody checks a brief before it goes online. When a mistake is found afterwards, {{ site.author.split(" ")[0] }} fixes it and the page is published again with a correction: the time at the top of the brief ("Corrected 09:14") and a note saying what changed, under "How this brief was made". A brief is never changed silently.

Each brief ends with "How this brief was made": the sources checked, how many items were collected, kept and shown, and every source or step that failed. If one source fails, the rest of the brief is still published and the failure is shown.

Severity rules

High
Directly affects Rotterdam operations and has a timeframe. Example: a strike at a terminal this week.
Medium
Affects a European route, carrier or hinterland connection that serves Rotterdam.
Low
Background trend or long-term news, or something already over when the brief is made (a closure lifted, a delay cleared). A port notice to shipping whose end date has passed is always Low, whatever the AI answered.
Unrated
The AI step failed for this item, so it has no severity, summary or impact: only the headline and a link. Unrated items come after all rated ones.

The impact for planners is always an interpretation, which is why it is set in a different typeface. When the source does not support one, the item says Not enough information instead of guessing.

Items from headline-only sources (see Sources) have no summary: the source is paywalled, or its terms don't allow one. The AI rates them from the headline alone, and the item says severity rated from the headline.

If the AI is unavailable all morning, the job tries again an hour later. If it is still down at the last try, a reduced brief is published so no day is skipped: the port status, and only items from the Rotterdam-specific sources, all unrated, under the note "The AI step was unavailable this morning: headlines only, no summaries." The strike flag then shows No data, because it can't be judged without the AI.

Weather warnings

Weather warnings are issued by KNMI, the Dutch national weather service, and read once a day, at around 06:45 Amsterdam time, from www.meteoalarm.org. They are not live: the weather dial says "Warnings at 06:45 · not live" and links to www.meteoalarm.org for the current warnings. Each reading shows KNMI's own level and hazard next to the level it maps to (for example "KNMI: Code yellow · Wind → Watch") and the time of issue of the warning. Only the level and the hazard are shown, never the warning text.

How the strike flag works

Strikes are announced days ahead, so the flag doesn't look at today's news alone. When the AI summarises an item about a strike, it notes where the strike is and whether it is announced, ongoing or called off. It copies the words that give its dates, and the code works out the dates from them. A date the source doesn't state is never guessed. Each morning the flag reads these details from the kept items of the last 14 days, plus any strike dated today or later from up to 60 days back.

  • Several items about the same strike: the newest one decides. A cancellation brings the flag back to Normal.
  • A false alarm is better than a missed strike. Only an item the AI confidently matches to a strike can lower its level. When the match is unsure, the strike stays up and both items are shown, so the reader can judge.
  • A strike reported as ongoing with no newer report after two days drops to Watch ("no newer report"), never to Normal by itself.
  • The flag links the item that decided it, with its source and date.

The AI models

  • Relevance check: {{ method.models.filter }}, one short yes-or-no question per item that the rules couldn't decide.
  • Summaries: {{ method.models.summary }}, chosen after a side-by-side test with {{ method.models.fallback }} on the same items. All kept items go to Anthropic's Batch API, which costs half as much. Items with no answer after {{ method.models.batch_wait_minutes }} minutes are summarised by {{ method.models.fallback }} instead, so the brief isn't held up. Each item records which model wrote it.
  • The AI picks places and "affects" tags only from fixed lists. Anything else it answers is dropped.
  • Every answer is checked in code. A summary that copies ten or more words in a row from the article, or runs too long, gets one more try. If the answer still fails, or the AI refuses, the item is shown unrated. No second model is asked to try instead.
  • All AI calls together are kept under €5 a month.

Sources

Every source was tested before use: does it have a feed or a readable page, what do its terms and robots.txt allow, and does it opt out of AI use? Each source's terms and robots.txt are respected. A source that opts out of AI use is headline-only: its article text never goes to an AI, only its headline and link are used.

Copyright and article text

  • Every item links to its original source. Summaries are short and written in the AI's own words, with no long quotes and no invented facts, numbers or quotes.
  • No article text is stored. An article is read in memory to write its summary, then discarded. What is stored, in public, is the headline, the link, the date and our own summary, so the original can always be reopened.
  • Paywalled sources, and sources whose terms allow links only, appear as a headline and link only.
  • Dutch articles get an English summary and a Read in English link that opens the original through Google Translate. Translated articles are never stored or republished here.

Privacy

  • This site sets no cookies and stores nothing in your browser: no cookies, no local storage, no session storage, no IndexedDB.
  • No analytics or tracking of any kind: no visitor counters, pixels or embedded content from other sites.
  • Everything a page needs is served from this site: the fonts, the styles, the scripts, Leaflet and the map. The map is drawn in your browser from a file prepared once from OpenStreetMap data, so no map service is asked for tiles. Opening a page makes your browser contact no other server.
  • Links are not loads. Links to the sources, OpenStreetMap, MeteoAlarm and GitHub contact those sites only when you click them. The Read in English link opens the original article through Google Translate, which then receives the article's address.
  • The site is hosted on GitHub Pages. GitHub logs the IP address of every visitor for security (GitHub Pages: data collection; GitHub Privacy Statement). Haven Daily itself collects no personal data.

The chart

  • The land and water are drawn from OpenStreetMap data. They are prepared once by a script, so the page does not depend on a map service. Roads, railways and storage tanks are drawn as texture, not as a road map.
  • The fine lines in the water are decoration: each one is a fixed distance from the shore. They are not depth contours and say nothing about where ships can sail.
  • Numbered squares on the chart are the day's items, placed at a location picked from a fixed list of port places.

  • Ship positions come from a short AIS snapshot of the port area, recorded each morning: where ships were at that moment, not live. Only seagoing cargo ships are shown (container ships, tankers and bulk carriers), not barges, tugs or pilot boats. Ships at a terminal named in an item are drawn in that item's colour.
  • Destinations are typed in by ship crews and are often messy, for example ANTWERP>HAMBURG or blank. The page shows a cleaned destination next to the original text, and Unknown when it is unclear.
